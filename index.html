How a build works

Three stages. You can stop after the first and still come out ahead.

Discovery

Two weeks. I sit with the people who do the work today, then write it down: what the system has to do, what it connects to, what could go wrong, and what it will cost to build.

You own that document whether or not you hire me for the build. Take it to another firm and it will still be the best specification anyone there has ever been handed.

The build, at a fixed price

Priced from the specification, agreed in writing, before a line of code is written. If it takes twice as long as I expected, the price doesn't change. That risk is mine, and carrying it is a real part of what you're paying for.

Then you run it, or I do

Either way, you already own it. The source lives in your own source repositories and the infrastructure in your cloud subscription from the first week — not handed over at the end, but yours while the work is happening.

So hosting is a convenience you buy, not a dependency you acquire. There's no version of this where you're stuck with me, and that's in the contract rather than promised on a website.

The questions you're too polite to ask

How does one person do this — and what happens if I get hit by a bus?

The first is about capability. A system like this once meant four developers and most of a year; it now takes weeks, because I've spent the past six months building the tooling that makes it possible — infrastructure that runs AI coding agents at volume behind enforced test, review, and merge gates, so work moves fast without anyone losing track of what changed or why.1 That's what lets a one-person practice quote a fixed price and mean it.

What the tooling can't do is decide what to build, notice the requirement nobody thought to mention, or take responsibility when something turns out to be wrong. That part is still a person, and here it's the same person throughout — in discovery, during the build, and on the phone eighteen months later.

The second question is the fair one, and most people are too polite to ask it out loud. The answer is structural rather than reassuring.

You own everything from the first week. Not at handover — from the start.

The source sits in your own source repositories. The infrastructure sits in your own cloud subscription. The tests, the deployment automation, the documentation, the keys — all in your possession while the work is still going on. So the worst case isn't that your system dies with me. It's that you hire someone to pick up a documented, tested, running codebase — and that is an ordinary thing to hire for.

Compare that honestly to the alternative. An agency's continuity is a promise about staffing — and when their senior engineer leaves, which happens, you find that nobody remaining understands your system either, and you hold no source, no deployment, and no leverage. Mine is a promise about what is already sitting in your accounts.
